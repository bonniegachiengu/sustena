On a warm morning in late May, half a honeybee colony walks out of its hive and settles on a branch as a hanging beard of ten thousand bees. It carries only the honey in its stomachs — about three days of it. In that time it must find a new home and move in. If the home is wrong, the colony does not survive the winter.

Nobody in the swarm knows where that home is. The queen is buried in the cluster and has no say. No bee has seen more than one candidate. What the swarm has is a few hundred scouts, flying out over thirty square kilometres, each inspecting exactly one hollow — walking its interior, measuring volume, entrance, height, draught — and returning with a private opinion about a place none of her sisters has visited. Out of those unshared, unverifiable opinions the swarm picks one site, usually the best available, and flies to it as a body.

Lindauer began working out how in the 1950s; Seeley finished the job on an island off Maine. The answer is not that some bee takes charge.

---

#### **The dance whose vigour is the argument**

A scout returns and dances. Direction and distance are the famous part; the part that matters here is **length** — the number of waggle runs is proportional to how good she judged the site. She does not argue. She advertises at an intensity that reflects her own assessment. And nobody takes her word for it: bees recruited by her dance fly out and inspect the place themselves.

Then two mechanisms that look like housekeeping and are the whole design.

**Decay.** Each time a scout returns she dances a little less, and eventually stops. Support is not a stock that accumulates; it must be re-earned by fresh independent inspections. A mediocre site runs out of advocates. Nobody has to argue it down.

**Cross-inhibition.** Seeley and colleagues showed in 2012 that a scout dancing for one site delivers brief stop signals — a head-butt with a vibratory pulse — to bees dancing for a *rival* site, never her own. It breaks ties, and it prevents the one outcome a swarm cannot survive: splitting in half.

The commitment rule is the strangest part. The swarm does not vote in the cluster. Scouts at a site count the *other scouts at that site*, and when a couple of dozen are present at once — a quorum, not a majority — that group returns and pipes, ten thousand bees warm their flight muscles, and the swarm launches toward a place almost none of them has seen.

Weighted advocacy from a private assessment. Independent re-inspection. Decay, so nothing survives on inertia. Mutual inhibition, so the body cannot hang. A threshold that ends deliberation before the honey runs out. A complete social-choice machine, and no chair.

#### **A library of answers to questions nobody has asked**

Before you meet a pathogen, your body has already made the antibody — millions of shapes, shuffled from a modest set of gene segments, each carried on its own lymphocyte, each waiting for a molecule that may never arrive.

When the pathogen arrives it instructs nothing. It **selects**. The few cells whose receptor happens to fit are stimulated to divide, and their descendants fill the field. That is Burnet's clonal selection theory, and it replaced an era of thinking in which the invader was supposed to teach the body what to build. Then the better half: in the germinal centres the copying is deliberately sloppy, descendants mutate their receptors and are re-selected on fit, and across days the population's grip measurably tightens.

Nothing here is design. It is a population of guesses, a test against the world, and retention of whatever passed — acquisition is mutation, the environment selects, the best model is retained.

And the structural point: the intelligence is not in any cell. No lymphocyte is clever. The cleverness is in keeping a diverse population and letting the world choose. Dawkins named the cultural version of a thing that copies, varies, and is differentially retained a **meme**, and the word fits a strategy better than it usually fits anything.

#### **The bird that must do two incompatible things at once**

A bird picking a seed out of gravel needs an attention that is narrow, sharp and grasping: *this* seed, not that stone. The same bird, in the same second, needs an attention that is wide and expecting nothing in particular, or a hawk takes it while it eats.

These are not two birds. McGilchrist's argument is that they are two modes inside one animal, held apart because the same machinery cannot do both at once, and that everything depends on which is finally in charge. His title is the parable: the Master, who sees the whole, and the Emissary, sent out to do a narrow job, who comes to believe his narrow view is the world.

As a design note it says something sharp. An agent with only the narrow attention is a superb maximiser inside a frame it can never notice is wrong. It will get the seed while the hawk arrives.

And the fix is not to hire a broad agent and seat it on the committee. That produces a council of half-minds, each incapable alone. The two attentions must live in the same head, because the entire job of the broad one is to interrupt the narrow one.

Hold that sentence exactly. The next one does not weaken it. It enlarges it.

#### **The same argument, at every size**

Ask how long the coast of Britain is and you get a question back: measured with what? A hundred-kilometre ruler gives one number; ten kilometres turns every bay into a detour and the number rises; one kilometre does it again inside every bay. Mandelbrot's point is that the number never settles. The coast is not a shape with a length finer instruments approach. It is a shape whose *character* is the same at every magnification.

Nature does this cheaply. A fern frond is made of smaller fronds; a river's tributaries branch like the river; your bronchi divide about twenty-three times on one plan. When something works at one scale, the pattern is reused a scale down, because reuse is nearly free and invention is not.

Now apply that to the bird. Broad and narrow are not only two attentions inside one animal. They are one relationship — an integrating, top-down mode that holds the whole, and a focused, bottom-up mode that does the specific work — and it reappears wherever there is more than one level. Inside one Symbiont, it is McGilchrist's pair. Between a Symbiont and its specialists, it is the same pair: the Symbiont holds the case, the specialists grind one part each. And between Orchie and the Council it is the same pair at the top of the household — Orchie the broad pole, perception and orchestration, the only thing that ever reaches a person; the Council and sub-Symbionts the narrow pole, specialised and *emergent*, in that what they collectively conclude is written down nowhere in advance. Super-consciousness and sub-consciousness, and the point is that they are one organism.

Then the part that is easy to get wrong. Saying the pattern repeats at household level does **not** licence building the household out of half-minds. Both claims hold, because they are about different things. *Every Symbiont has both attentions* is a claim about agents. *Every level has a broad pole and a narrow pole* is a claim about levels. Orchie being the broad pole does not make Orchie a creature without narrow attention — it must score its routing sharply, and the Council must scan broadly enough to notice the frame has moved. Read the second claim as permission to skip the first and you have rebuilt the council of half-minds the first exists to forbid.

McGilchrist's warning survives the change of scale in both directions. The Emissary usurping the Master, one level up, is a committee's aggregate score quietly overruling the view of the whole. The mirror failure is the Master's: a summary so clean that what the specialists saw does not survive into it.

One more claim, deliberately unglamorous. A sub-Symbiont — a specialist nested inside a Symbiont — **is not a new kind of thing.** A Symbiont already carries a model of itself, and that self-model is a Sustain: its own boundary, state, limits, moves. So a Symbiont inside a Symbiont is a Sustain inside a Sustain: the composition the very first article described, used once more. Not a new primitive. The old one, again.

Mandelbrot is the intuition. Composition is the mechanism. Keep them apart — the fractal explains why it looks like this; it does none of the work.

#### **The animal that changes the question**

A beaver fells trees, drags them, and dams a stream. On any honest short-run accounting this is a disaster: enormous energy, nothing eaten, long exposed hours on land where a beaver is least safe.

What it buys is not a reward. It is a pond, and a pond is a different set of possibilities — lodge entrances opening under water where nothing can follow, a floating haulage route, a larder of sunken branches in water that stays liquid under ice. None of those moves existed before the dam. The animal did not get better at its environment; it changed which environment it lives in.

Odling-Smee, Laland and Feldman named this **niche construction**: organisms are selected by their environments, and organisms build the environments that do the selecting. The pond outlasts the beaver, silting into a meadow other animals inherit.

Hold on to the accounting. There is a real, common, correct decision that any per-period maximiser refuses. Anything scoring only *how am I doing this month* says no to the dam every time, and is wrong every time.

The financial name for that accounting is **runway**. Not "am I profitable" but the count of months until, at current flows and with nothing changing, you are forced to stop — the liveable region of the first article with a clock attached. Which makes the shape obvious. Raising money is a dam: expensive, a lot of exposed hours not doing the work, no revenue. What it buys is months, and months are not a reward, they are options — the meetings you can take, the failures you can afford, the slow customer you can wait for. A scorer that only asks about this period refuses it confidently, and is wrong exactly as the beaver's critics would be.

#### **The pile that keeps its own slope**

Drop sand onto a table, one grain at a time. At first each grain stays where it lands. Then the sides steepen and grains slide. Add one and a few neighbours shift. Add one and nothing happens. Add one and a whole face lets go.

What the pile is doing is holding a slope — not one anyone chose, but one it walks to from either direction: too shallow and grains accumulate until it steepens, too steep and it avalanches until it relaxes. Bak, Tang and Wiesenfeld, 1987: **self-organised criticality**, feedback driving a system with nobody at the dial to precisely the state where it is maximally responsive.

At that state, the response to one more grain has no typical size. Most grains do nothing; some move a handful; very rarely one takes down the face. Plot how often each size occurs and you get not a bell curve but a straight line on log paper — a power law, the mathematical statement that *there is no such thing as a normal-sized avalanche*. The trigger carries no information about the outcome.

Two pieces of honesty. First, the sandpile was a computer model before it was a pile of sand, and real granular experiments were mixed — long-grain rice reproduced the power law, rounder grains did not. The durable claim is not "everything is a sandpile" but the mechanism: feedback can hold a system where the size of the response to a small push has no characteristic scale. Second, this is not an argument that nothing is knowable. It is an argument about *which* question is answerable. You cannot predict the avalanche. You can measure the slope.

The household version is not subtle. A household with no slack is a pile at its critical angle: every shilling committed, rent and fees and stock and a supplier expecting payment on the day. Then a customer pays late. On a good month that is an inconvenience. On this month it is a missed payment, which is a supplier who tightens terms, which is a lost discount, which is a thinner margin, which is the next payment missed. The shock was the same size both times. The slope was not.

So the Symbiont's job is not to forecast the cascade. It is to notice the slope — to say *this Sustain is in a state where a small shock does not stay small* — and to say it to a person before the grain lands.

There is a second, constructive use of the same dynamics. Simple feedback produces both order and chaos; the butterfly effect is the name for a rounding error in the start dominating the finish. That makes long forecasting hopeless and *generation* cheap. Run the same simple loop forward a few hundred times from slightly different starts and you get a spread of futures nobody would have thought to write down. With very little information, that is not noise. It is a scenario library, produced by the thing that made prediction hard.

Which leaves what to do while standing on a steep pile, and it has an old answer. Where cause and effect are analysable, analyse — that is what expertise is for. Where they are legible only in retrospect, run small experiments you can afford to lose and amplify what works. Where there is no discernible cause and effect at all — where the face is already sliding — act first, get order back, analyse afterwards. Those are Cynefin's response modes, and they are not interchangeable. Careful analysis during a collapse is not caution; it is the wrong instrument. Acting first where thinking would have found the answer is the same error with the sign flipped. So: a specialist should know which weather it is good in.

#### **A regulator has to be a model**

In 1970 Conant and Ashby proved a small hard theorem with a blunt title: **every good regulator of a system must be a model of that system.** Not "should be." A regulator that succeeds in holding a system to a narrow set of outcomes is, by that success, carrying a model of it. The regulation *is* the model, expressed.

A thermostat models a room with one number and one threshold, and holds one variable. A person keeping a household solvent through a bad month is running something far richer — what things cost, who gets paid when, which bills bend, what a delayed payment does to a relationship.

Then the recursion, which is where this article's subject lives. A regulator is itself a system, with limits: how fast it can act, what acting costs, what it has already committed. So it needs two models — one of the world it regulates, and one of itself doing the regulating. Without the second it will confidently promise moves it cannot make.

Machine learning arrived at the first from the other direction. Sutton's Dyna made the observation that an agent learning from real experience can also learn a *model* of its environment and practise against it, so one real observation pays for many imagined ones. Ha and Schmidhuber took it further in 2018: their agent compressed what it saw into a small internal world model, trained its policy almost entirely inside that model — inside its own dream — and transferred the policy back, where it worked.

That is exactly what this system calls a simulator: a shared, correctable model of the household every Symbiont can run forward before anything real happens. Sharing is the point — a correction to the model is a correction for everyone, once. But the same sentence contains the warning. A shared dream is a shared error. When every advisor runs its own scenario against one common model, they are independent in what they try and not at all independent in what they assume. The model's blind spot is in everyone's answer, and a room full of confident agreement is what that looks like from outside.

None of that was a metaphor, and this is where it lands.

The cell gave us a region a system must stay inside. The enzyme gave us the only legal moves. The law put a gate on every move and made the region binding. The record gave us the ordered history everything stands on. All four described a world — and **none contained anyone who wants anything.**

An **Symbiont** is the piece that wants something, and its definition is stingy on purpose. It is not a program with its own data and its own powers. It is `⟨uᵢ, Πᵢ, attentionᵢ, M_selfᵢ, M_worldᵢ⟩` over the **same** ⟨S, T⟩ every other Symbiont shares. N agents, one world, differing **only in what they want**.

Which is why they can be the named helpers: Mentor watches the money, Protégé the calendar, Attaché the people, Curator the things, Navigator the routes. Five points of view on one household — not five programs with five databases. Mentor cannot make a move Navigator could not. Adding a Symbiont can never enlarge what the system can do; it can only change how the same options rank.

Which is also why those five, plus Orchie, are simply **the default** — every household gets them, unconfigured, on day one. That would be reckless in almost any other architecture and here costs nothing, for the reason just given: one world, one set of legal moves, so there is no capability in the set that was not already there and no risk the gate would not already have refused. What arrives free is coverage: five life-domains watched from the first day by five different scoring cards. Orchie is there for a different reason — it is the only thing that reaches a person, and since nothing happens without a person, a household with no Orchie is not unsafe, it is silent. Everything beyond the six is optional, from a marketplace rather than from birth.

A **strategy** is the scout's flight plan: an operator-DAG, a shaped composition of moves that already exist. A strategy is a meme — and the amendment widens the word to four kinds worth copying: a **model** of how something works, a **strategy** over T, a **resource** held, a **network** to someone. **Learning is search over that space** — vary, test, keep what scored. The germinal centre, not the lecture. Symbionts are what such a search settles into: a stable way of wanting, holding and moving that keeps paying.

What turns that search from a one-time sweep into something alive is **feedback**. Every real result — a user's correction, a month that went differently than modelled, another Symbiont's objection — comes back and reconfigures the strategy that produced it. That loop is the oldest idea in the series, and it is also what the sandpile is made of: the same feedback that tunes a system into shape walks it to the edge of its own stability. Which is why watching the slope is a standing duty, not a special mode.

Testing happens where the record said it could — on a **fork**. A copy-on-write snapshot, replayed forward **under the same gate**, so a simulation can never explore a path the real household would have refused. Score it on what the Symbiont wants, on whether every step stays inside the region it can *keep* staying inside, and — the beaver clause — on how much it enlarges what becomes reachable afterwards. Runway is that middle term with a clock, and the reason a system can be told that a costly, unprofitable, exhausting month of raising money was right.

Each Symbiont also declares the weather it is built for. An advisor whose whole method is careful optimisation is right in a complicated month and wrong in a collapsing week, and the honest handling is not to hope it notices but to write down which regimes it suits and route around it in the others. That declaration is also the system admitting something: most of this machinery assumes the world is analysable in advance, and a household is not always that world.

Then the argument. Arrow proved in 1951 that there is no perfect way to turn several individual rankings into one group ranking. So Sustena does not look for one. **The weights are declared** — written down, visible, arguable — and the human holds the residual. And because a weighted sum silently picks one point on a trade-off surface and hides the rest, the council's job is to surface the **frontier** of options none of which beats another, and let a person choose. That is not a shortcoming of the mathematics. It is what the mathematics instructs.

Each Symbiont carries both attentions: the narrow one that scores a candidate, the broad one whose job is to notice the frame changed. Not narrow specialists and broad specialists on a committee — both, in each, and then the same pair one level up, between the one that orchestrates and the many that emerge. And each carries its `M_self` and `M_world`, because Conant and Ashby. `M_self` is a Sustain. The cell again, one level down.

There is a language model in here, and it sits on the proposal side only: it suggests, and everything it suggests is simulated and gated like anything else, so nothing is trusted for being fluent. It is used where deterministic methods genuinely fail — the messy long tail, an ambiguous sentence, a summary for a human — and nowhere else. Because it is a real cost and not a magic ingredient, it is **metered and budgeted**: every call measured, charged, and governed by a policy the user writes, per kind of task, with a ceiling. Turning it off for a class of work is not a switch labelled "AI"; it is a rule that this node may not run here, sending the strategy down a deterministic path or to a question for a person. Which model to use is the same kind of rule. The reason a budget may simply refuse it is that nothing in the system's correctness ever rested on it.

One more check, the quiet one. When every Symbiont pushes its own score, the dimensions in **nobody's** score are where damage collects unwatched — a perfect blood test and a weakening skeleton. So the un-optimised dimensions get an invariant of their own in V, enforced by the same gate as everything else.

Which leaves the sentence the module rests on.

The scouts fly, and they advertise, and they are usually right — and not one of them gets to move the swarm. In Sustena that is not a policy, a promise, or a well-behaved default. Nothing a Symbiont decides touches the world except through the gate every other move goes through, and the gate's condition includes a valid approval token from a person. **Simulated, then voted, then approved.** An unapproved effect is not punished, not logged as a violation, not reported on Friday. It is a move that does not exist.
