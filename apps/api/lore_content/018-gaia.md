In the autumn of 1965, a chemist sat in a corridor at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Pasadena and worked out how to detect life on Mars without going there — and in doing so accidentally answered a question about your kitchen.

James Lovelock had been hired to help design instruments for the Mars landers. The other teams were building beautiful machines: scoops that would lift Martian soil into little chambers, nutrient broths, detectors watching for the gases a growing microbe would exhale. Lovelock asked the awkward question at the meeting. *What are you looking for, exactly?* Life like ours, in soil like ours, eating food like ours. If Mars had built something else, every one of those instruments would come back negative and none of them would be wrong.

So he went the other way, and the move he made is the reason this article exists.

Don't look for organisms. Look at the *atmosphere*. A dead planet's air settles, over geological time, into chemical equilibrium — everything that can react has reacted, and what's left is ash. Mars is carbon dioxide, and it has been carbon dioxide for a very long time, and it is going nowhere. Nothing is pushing.

Then he looked at Earth's air, and it is an absurdity.

Our atmosphere contains oxygen at about a fifth of everything, and methane, at the same time. Those two do not coexist. Methane burns in oxygen; left alone, a molecule of it survives roughly a decade before it is oxidised away. To find both in the same air, in stable amounts, century after century, is like walking into a room and finding a fire that has been burning for four billion years with no one carrying wood.

Something is carrying wood. Continuously. At a rate that holds.

**You can see life from orbit,** Lovelock realised, **not as a thing but as a difference that is being maintained** — a disequilibrium held steady against the pull of chemistry, by work that never stops.

That is not a new sentence in this series. It is the first one. It is the sodium gradient across the wall of every cell in your body, the one that dissolves the moment the pumps stop. Lovelock had found the same object at a scale eight orders of magnitude larger and had not yet noticed.

---

#### **The four-billion-year band**

Once you start looking for the holding, it is everywhere in the rock record, and some of it is genuinely difficult to explain away.

Start with the Sun, which is not a constant. Stars brighten as they age; the standard solar model says our Sun was something like a quarter to a third dimmer when the Earth formed, and has been climbing ever since. Run the arithmetic on a planet with today's atmosphere and that young Sun, and the Earth should have been frozen solid for its first two billion years. Carl Sagan and George Mullen wrote it up in 1972 and it has been called the faint young Sun paradox ever since, because the rocks flatly refuse to cooperate: there is sedimentary evidence of liquid water at least 3.8 billion years ago, and life not long after. The oceans were never frozen for long. The Sun got a third brighter and the water stayed liquid the whole way.

Then oxygen. Today it sits at about 21%, and that number is not comfortable — it is *narrow*. Much below the high teens and a fire won't propagate through vegetation at all. Push it much above the mid-twenties and the argument, first made by Lovelock and Margulis and Andrew Watson and still argued over in the details, is that fires would spread through damp forest, and forests would stop being a thing that exists. And there is a check on it: charcoal. Fossil charcoal appears continuously in the rock record from roughly 420 million years ago onward, which means fires have been possible, without interruption, for four hundred million years — so oxygen never fell below the fire threshold in all that time. Combined with the fact that we still have forests, it never rose far above it either.

Four hundred million years inside a band a few percentage points wide, through continental collisions, asteroid impacts, five mass extinctions and an ice age or two.

Now say what that shape is, because you already know. It is not a target the planet is hitting. It is a **region it has stayed inside**, and staying inside it is the entire job. Blood pH between 7.35 and 7.45. Core temperature within a fraction of a degree of 37. Atmospheric oxygen between a fire that won't start and a fire that won't stop.

Same shape. Different scale. Nothing else changed.

#### **The hypothesis, and the novelist who named it**

Lovelock lived in a village in Wiltshire, and his neighbour was William Golding, who had written *Lord of the Flies*. Lovelock described the idea to him on a walk — that the Earth's living matter, its air, its oceans and its surface rock might behave as a single self-regulating system — and said he needed a name for it. Golding, who had read Classics, said: call it Gaia.

The idea needed a second author, and it got exactly the right one.

Lynn Margulis had spent the 1960s being told she was wrong about something else. Her argument, published in 1967 and expanded into a book in 1970, was that the complex cell — the kind you and every plant and every fungus are made of — is not a single organism at all but a *confederation*: that mitochondria were once free-living bacteria that ended up inside a larger cell and were never digested, and are still in there, still wrapped in their own membranes, still carrying their own genomes, still dividing on their own schedule. It took years of resistance and eventually the genetics settled it in her favour, and it is now in every textbook.

So when Lovelock came to her with a planet that behaved like an organism, she was the least surprised person in science. She had already shown that an organism could *be a committee that stayed a committee*. Her framing of Gaia was characteristically blunt and much better than the mystical version that later got attached to it: **Gaia is symbiosis seen from space.**

Their joint paper landed in *Tellus* in 1974, under a title that says exactly what it means and no more — *Atmospheric homeostasis by and for the biosphere*.

#### **The objection, which is a good one**

Here is where an honest article has to slow down, because the criticism that arrived was not a misunderstanding. It was correct, and it nearly killed the idea.

Ford Doolittle put it first, in 1981, and Richard Dawkins put it hardest in *The Extended Phenotype* the following year. Natural selection is not a wish. It is a mechanism, and it requires very specific ingredients: a population of entities, variation among them, heredity, and differential reproduction. Give it those and adaptations appear. Withhold any one and nothing happens, ever.

Now count the planets. There is one Earth. It has no siblings, no offspring, and no ancestors. There has never been a population of Earths, some of which regulated their temperature well and left more descendants than the ones that didn't. So *by what process* could the Earth have acquired the ability to look after itself? Individual organisms are selected to out-reproduce their neighbours, not to stabilise a global thermostat that benefits their competitors equally. A microbe that spent energy cooling the planet would be outcompeted within a season by one that didn't.

Dawkins's version was lethal and short: Gaia asks you to believe in an adaptation with no possible history.

James Kirchner added the methodological knife a few years later, and it still stings. Gaia, he pointed out, is not one hypothesis but a stack of them, ranging from the trivially true (life influences the environment — nobody disputes this) to the untestable (the biosphere *optimises* conditions for itself). The weak versions are safe and say little. The strong versions say a great deal and can't be checked. And an idea that quietly slides down the stack when challenged, then climbs back up when unobserved, is not doing science.

That criticism landed. For most of the 1980s, Gaia was something respectable geochemists avoided at parties.

#### **Daisyworld — regulation with nobody in charge**

The reply came in 1983, and it is one of the most elegant things I know, because it is not an argument. It is a toy.

Andrew Watson and Lovelock built the simplest imaginable planet and let it run. Call it Daisyworld. It orbits a star that gets steadily brighter, exactly as ours does. It has no clouds, no oceans, no continents, no chemistry to speak of. It has two species of daisy.

Black daisies and white daisies. That's it.

Each daisy is entirely selfish and entirely stupid. It has one preference: it grows best at a certain temperature, poorly above and below, and dies outside a range. It knows nothing about the planet. It has no goals. It cannot sense the star.

But black daisies are dark, so they absorb sunlight and warm the ground around them. White daisies are pale, so they reflect it and cool the ground around them. That is the only global consequence of a purely local act.

Now turn the star up slowly and watch.

Early on the planet is cold, so the black daisies do better — they warm their own patches, and the more of them there are, the warmer the whole planet gets, until it is warm enough for daisies generally. As the star keeps brightening, the extra heat starts to *disadvantage* black daisies and favour white ones. The population shifts. More white ground, more reflection, less absorbed heat. The planet's temperature barely moves.

Across an enormous range of stellar brightness — a range over which a bare rock would have roasted — Daisyworld's surface temperature stays almost flat. And then, when the star gets bright enough that even a planet covered entirely in white daisies cannot shed the heat, the regulation fails, the daisies die, and the temperature shoots up to what physics said it should have been all along.

Read what just happened, because it is the entire answer to Dawkins.

**No daisy did anything for the planet.** Not one of them sacrificed a single seed for the common good. There was no group selection, no planetary lineage, no foresight, no purpose, and no mechanism more exotic than "things that like it warmer do better when it's cold." The regulation was not an adaptation that had to evolve. It was **arithmetic** — the automatic consequence of local competition among agents whose local acts happen to have global side effects that feed back on them.

That is the honest form of Gaia, and it survives. Not *the Earth wants to live*. Rather: **when the parts of a system act only for themselves, but their actions change the conditions they act under, regulation can fall out for free — and when it does, no one is steering.**

The most recent serious work goes further and answers the "no population of planets" objection in a way I find quietly devastating. Tim Lenton and colleagues, writing in 2018, point out that you do not need planets to reproduce. You need configurations to *persist*. A biosphere-atmosphere arrangement that destabilises itself is, by construction, short-lived. One that damps its own perturbations lasts. Run that filter for four billion years and what you are left with looking at, necessarily, is a configuration that held — not because it was selected *for* holding, but because everything that didn't hold isn't here to be observed. They call it selection by survival alone, and it needs no daisies, no altruism, and no mother.

#### **The honest part, which is not comfortable**

I want to put down clearly what this does and does not license, because the popular version of Gaia is sentimental and the real one is not.

The mechanisms that hold up best are the boring ones. The strongest is the silicate weathering thermostat, worked out by James Walker, Paul Hays and James Kasting in 1981, and it is mostly geology: when the planet warms, it rains more and rock weathers faster, and weathering pulls carbon dioxide out of the air, which cools the planet. When it cools, weathering slows, volcanic CO₂ accumulates, and the planet warms. A genuine negative feedback loop with a timescale of hundreds of thousands of years — and one that would work, more weakly, on a sterile Earth.

What life does is *amplify* it, enormously. Roots crack rock. Soil microbes acidify it. And — this is the detail I cannot resist — the mycorrhizal fungi from the last article, the ones trading phosphorus for carbon in the dark under a forest, are among the most aggressive rock-weathering agents on the planet. The wood-wide web is part of the planetary thermostat. The exact size of the amplification is still argued over. That it is large is not.

Meanwhile, some of the prettier Gaian mechanisms have not aged well. The CLAW hypothesis of 1987 — marine algae emitting sulphur compounds that seed clouds that reflect sunlight that cools the ocean the algae live in — was beautiful and is now largely regarded as too weak to regulate anything, after Patricia Quinn and Timothy Bates went through the evidence in 2011 and found the loop wasn't there at the strength required.

And then the part that ends the sentimentality outright. **Life is not a caretaker.** The single largest environmental catastrophe in Earth's history was caused by the biosphere: around 2.4 billion years ago, cyanobacteria that had learned to split water dumped free oxygen into an atmosphere that had none, and oxygen is corrosive. The Great Oxidation Event poisoned the anaerobic world that was there first and remade the planet's chemistry over the objections of almost everything alive at the time. Later, the planet froze over — possibly to the equator — for stretches of the Cryogenian. There have been five mass extinctions, and the worst of them, at the end of the Permian, killed something on the order of three-quarters or more of everything in the sea.

Gaia is not benevolent. Gaia is not looking after anyone. What Gaia is, exactly and only, is **persistent** — a system that has, so far, kept a handful of critical variables inside the bands where the whole arrangement continues to function, using feedbacks no one designed, and has occasionally come very close to failing.

That is not a lesser claim. It is the *right* claim, and it is the one that transfers, because a household is not benevolent either. A household is a system holding some numbers inside some bands, imperfectly, at cost, without a plan, mostly by feedbacks nobody wrote down. And it can fail. And when it does, nothing dramatic arrives — the holding just stops.

#### **Now say it plainly**

None of that was a metaphor, and here is where the series has been going since the first article.

A Sustain in Sustena is written **Σ = ⟨B, S, V, T, ⊕⟩**. Read the planet again with those five letters in your hand.

**B is the boundary.** Not a wall — the atmosphere shades off into space over hundreds of kilometres — but a definite inside: the air, the ocean, the soil and the weathering crust, the region in which the chemistry is not the chemistry of the vacuum. What is inside is what the system is responsible for holding.

**S is the state.** Surface temperature. Atmospheric oxygen, carbon dioxide, methane. Ocean pH and salinity. Ice volume, albedo, biomass. The complete description of what is true inside the boundary right now — and, like bone, the folded-up total of everything that ever happened to it. Which is why you can read it out of ice cores and rock and tree rings: **state is the fold of the log**, and the planet keeps very good records.

**V is the viable region.** Liquid water. Oxygen between the fire that won't start and the fire that won't stop. Not a target the Earth is aiming at — a region it is inside. And it is not the same as being *safe*: the Cryogenian was inside V by the skin of its teeth, and the end-Permian tested it hard.

**T is the transitions.** Photosynthesis, respiration, weathering, burial, volcanism, subduction. The moves by which state may change, every one of them with a rate, every one of them paid for. Holding still is the expensive option here too.

**⊕ is composition.** And this is the letter Margulis wrote. The biosphere is made of organisms; the organisms are made of cells; the cells contain bacteria that were never digested. Every rung is a whole with an inside, and every rung is a part of the rung above — and **the whole is real, and it never erases the parts inside it.** The Earth does not get to overwrite a bacterium's genome to balance its own books, any more than a household total gets to overwrite a person's pocket.

And homeostasis — the thing Lovelock and Margulis actually named in that 1974 title — is what ties T to V. Choose the moves that reduce the distance to the region you must stay inside. That is a Controller. It has been running for four billion years with no controller in it.

**Gaia is a Sustain.** Not like one. One.

#### **All the way up, all the way down**

Now do the zoom, because the zoom is the thesis.

**A cell.** Five nanometres of membrane, an inland sea it keeps at concentrations the outside world does not have, a viable band per variable, a pump on every gradient, and a bacterium inside it that kept its own books.

**A person.** Cells into tissue into organs into you, one temperature to defend, one bloodstream to keep in range, one way to die. In Sustena you are a **habitat** — the person-level Sustain, the smallest complete unit, the thing that gets credited and debited, and the thing that never dissolves into anything it joins.

**A household.** A **homestead**: rent due Thursday, a fridge with three days in it, a phone that buzzes at 14:40 with a message about money that moved at 14:02. Its boundary is who is in it. Its state is the balances and the stock and the calendar. Its viable region is the set of conditions under which everyone eats and the rent lands. Its transitions are the spends and the transfers and the shopping. And its composition is the members' habitats, folded up and never overwritten.

**A business.** A kitchen holding a season. A float, a shelf of stock, a supplier who wants paying before the customer does. Same five letters. A different band.

**A chama.** Money pooled by people who did not have to trust an institution to do it, which is the oldest working proof that a group can be a Sustain with a treasury and a rulebook and no software at all.

**A network.** The forest floor from the last article: **Mycelium**, whose members are Sustains, whose boundary is membership, whose state includes a treasury, whose transitions route and publish and settle. Not a layer above the trees — more roots and more fungus, the same biology at a bigger scale. And with a market riding on it, the **Arena**, which is also a Sustain, whose members are the things for sale.

**A biosphere.** Gaia. Everything above, composed, holding an oxygen band for four hundred million years.

And now keep going, because the recursion does not politely stop at the edge of the atmosphere.

**A star.** The Sun is a homeostat, and this is not a stretch — it is why it has not exploded. Its core is a self-gravitating gas held between two opposed forces: gravity pulling in, pressure from fusion pushing out. The fusion rate is fiercely sensitive to temperature. So if the burning speeds up, the core heats, and the extra heat makes it *expand*, and expansion cools it, and cooling slows the burning back down. If the burning slows, the core contracts, and contraction heats it, and the burning picks up. A negative feedback loop, running for four and a half billion years, with a gain sharp enough that the Sun's output is stable to a fraction of a percent.

Its boundary is the photosphere. Its state is mass, composition, core temperature, luminosity. Its viable region has a name astronomers already use: **the main sequence** — the band a star occupies for as long as it can hold itself. And its death is exactly what the first article said death is. Nothing arrives. The core hydrogen runs out, the feedback that held it breaks, and the holding stops.

**A solar system.** With a boundary you can point at and a date it was crossed: the heliopause, where the Sun's wind finally gives out against the pressure of the galaxy. Voyager 1 went through it in August 2012, about 121 times further from the Sun than we are. Instruments on board recorded the plasma density jump. A membrane, measured, from the inside.

**A galaxy.** Here I want to be careful, because this is where a lesser article would start singing. A galaxy is unquestionably a bounded, composed, self-gravitating system with a state and a set of transitions. Whether it *regulates* itself in Gaia's sense is a much weaker and more contested claim. But there is at least one real feedback loop in it: star formation is self-limiting, because the massive stars that form die quickly and violently, and supernovae heat and blow apart the cold gas that would otherwise have collapsed into the next generation of stars. Make stars too fast and you destroy your own raw material. That is a negative feedback on a galactic budget, it is well attested, and it is the same shape as everything above.

**And below?** The recursion runs down as far as you like *in the formalism*: a household contains a treasury contains a pocket; a farm contains a field contains a soil sensor holding a moisture band. Whether physical reality has a smallest self-maintaining thing is a question about chemistry that this framework does not claim to answer. What it claims is narrower and stronger:

**Wherever you cut the ladder, the object you find has the same five parts.** There is no distinguished floor and no distinguished ceiling. Every Sustain is a whole with an inside, and every Sustain is a part of something larger, and both are true simultaneously, and that is not a coincidence to be admired — it is the one structural fact the entire system is built on.

#### **The name**

So now the name, which is the thing this article was always going to have to explain.

**Sustena** comes from two words: *sustenance* and *sustainability*. They sound like they belong to different conversations — one is dinner, the other is a conference panel — and they are in fact the same word wearing different clothes. Both descend from the Latin *sustinēre*: *sub*, from underneath, plus *tenēre*, to hold. **To hold up from below.**

Sustenance is what holds a body up from one hour to the next. Sustainability is what holds a system up from one decade to the next. It is one act at two timescales, and the root of both is the thing this framework is built out of:

**Sustain.**

It is a verb pressed into service as a noun, and that is deliberate, because the object is not a thing. The cell taught us this in the first article and Maturana and Varela gave it a name: a thing that holds itself together is not a substance, it is a *process that includes making its own boundary*. Nearly every molecule in you will be replaced. What persists is the holding. Call the object a Sustain, then, because naming it after the act is more honest than naming it after the stuff.

And Sustena is the name of the whole — the mother-Sustain, in Gaia's exact sense. Not a platform the Sustains sit on. Not a layer of management above them. **Sustena is itself a Sustain, whose members are all the Sustains inside it**, and which is in turn a member of larger ones: an economy, a society, a biosphere, a planet going round a slowly brightening star.

The last article said there was exactly one Sustain still above the network. This is it. And the honest thing to say about it is the thing we just said about Gaia: it is not benevolent, and it is not above its members. It has a boundary — what has been declared into it. It has a state — every household and business and pocket and sensor inside it. It has a viable region — the conditions under which the whole thing stays trustworthy and usable and worth running. It has transitions, and every one of them is gated. And it composes upward into things it does not control, the way a mitochondrion composes into a cell that cannot read its genome.

#### **Everything is named after the thing that solved it first**

The rest of the vocabulary is a nod, and I want to state the reason before the list, because it is not whimsy.

**Every hard problem in this system was solved, at least once, by something with no designer, no budget and no deadline, several billion years before anyone wrote it down.** Naming the parts after the originals is not decoration. It is a citation.

A **Niche** is the viable region seen as a place to live — and the word is not borrowed loosely. G. Evelyn Hutchinson, in 1957, defined an ecological niche as an *n*-dimensional hypervolume: one axis per environmental variable, and the region within it where a species can persist. That is V, exactly, defined by an ecologist thirty years before anyone needed it for software.

A **Genome** is a Sustain's written definition — the full declared ⟨S, V, T⟩ — because a genome is a description that gets read into a running thing and is never consumed by the reading.

A **Gene** and a **Meme** sit side by side, and this is where the naming convention earns its most interesting rule. Richard Dawkins coined *meme* in 1976 for a unit of culture that replicates, varies and is selected — a second replicator, running on the same logic as the first but in a different medium. **Culture is also nature.** A strategy a Symbiont carries and passes on is doing exactly what a gene does; it deserves the same family of names, and it gets one.

An **Enzyme** is a guarded transition. Nothing happens until something says yes; the guard is specificity; the effect is catalysis; some are reversible and those are the ones you can be relaxed about.

A **Symbiont** is an agent that lives *inside* a Sustain in mutualism, senses, proposes, learns — and never decides alone. Margulis's word, and Margulis's point: the thing inside kept its own membrane, and the thing around it never got to overrule it.

A **spore** is a forkable seed that germinates into a new Sustain, which is what a spore is.

**Mycelium** is the network, because the forest floor is not a management layer, it is more roots.

The **Arena** is the market, because biological markets are real — plants and fungi discriminate on price, sanction under-delivery, and have been running supply and demand in soil, with no money and no judges, since long before we invented either.

**Pawa** is energy, the ATP of the system, metered per thought. **Juul** is the coin. **Embroidery** is the language you author in, because nature does not rewrite the organism — it stitches new patterns onto working base definitions, which is the only safe way anything complicated has ever been changed. **Orchie** is the curated eye that shows you only what earned a slot. A **Habitat** is a person; a **Homestead** is a household. **Tolerance** is the immune posture, and the immunologists were right that the hard problem is restraint.

And there is a rule underneath the whole list. Where a term has a technical origin — *operator*, *operative*, *DSL* — **the product and the writing use the nature name, and the code keeps the technical name as an alias until the rename lands.** That is not indecision. It is the same discipline as everything else here: change the definition where it is authored, once, and let it propagate, rather than editing a running system in twelve places and hoping.

#### **What it is for**

So what is the whole thing *for*, now that it is assembled?

The claim is old and simple and it comes from the unified-framework paper that started all this: **any system that can be described can be modelled; anything that can be modelled can be simulated; anything that can be simulated can be planned against; and anything that can be planned can be run under a gate that refuses the moves that would take it outside its own declared region.**

Describe → model → simulate → optimise → execute → observe, and then round again, forever, which is the loop a cell runs and a thermostat runs and a warship's bridge runs.

Which means the same object, once it genuinely works, is not a household budgeting app that grew. It is a substrate for:

**A household holding a month.** A business holding a season. A farm with a soil sensor whose flat battery escalates exactly like an overdrawn pocket, because a dead sensor and an empty account are the same event — a Sustain leaving the region it declared.

**Sustainable projects**, in the literal sense — a project whose whole justification is a claim about staying inside a band, and which currently proves that claim with a PDF. A viable region is a machine-checkable version of the same promise.

**Logistics among the planets.** I am going to say the quiet part, because the vision is allowed one indulgence and this is it. The ships in *The Expanse* are, underneath the drama, ledgers with engines: air, water, reaction mass, food, crew hours, delta-v, all held inside bands, all composing into a fleet, all under a gate that has to refuse a burn that would leave you unable to stop. Life support **is** a viable region with a controller on it and a kernel underneath — the smaller inner region from which you can still get home. A framework that can hold a kitchen's stock and a household's month is not a different framework from that one. It is the same one, with harder numbers and less forgiving walls.

**Defence and life-support systems**, where the difference between "inside the safe region now" and "inside the region from which you can *stay* safe" is the entire discipline.

**Humane AI** — not as a slogan. As a structure: an agent that can propose anything and commit nothing; whose every suggestion is simulated, priced, voted on and gated; whose reach is metered so it cannot think its way past a budget; and where a person's approval is not a policy that could be relaxed but a term inside the check that lets a move exist at all. The scouts fly, and they advertise, and not one of them gets to move the swarm.

**Operations. Governance.** A council whose rules are constraints, whose votes are transitions, and whose decisions are in a log that cannot be quietly edited afterwards.

There is a name for why that list has no natural end. David Deutsch calls it *the beginning of infinity*: a system that can create and correct knowledge — hold a model, test it against the world, and repair it — has no upper bound to its reach except the laws of physics themselves. *Problems are inevitable; problems are soluble.* A framework whose entire job is to hold a thing in range, notice when it drifts, and correct the drift without breaking the thing is, in that precise sense, an error-correcting machine — and error-correcting machines are the ones that get to keep going, which is the whole reason the genetic code is digital and the ledger is append-only. The recursion having no top is not a flourish. It is the claim that there is no scale at which the method stops working.

And I should be honest about where this stands today, because this series does not do triumphant. What exists right now is a running application, a real ledger, a real gate, a real household, and eighteen pairs of articles that trace every piece of it back to something that was already true. The rest is a claim about the *shape*, not a report of what has shipped. The white paper that follows this one says exactly which is which, in detail, without flattering anybody.

#### **Back to the wall**

So: back to where the series started, five nanometres thick.

Outside the cell, sodium is abundant. Inside, it is not. Nothing about chemistry wants that, and the difference is not a fact — it is a fight being won, molecule by molecule, every second, at a cost of something like a third of everything the cell has, for as long as it is alive. And when it dies, nothing is taken away. The pumps stop. The difference dissolves into the surroundings.

Four billion years and twenty-six orders of magnitude away, an atmosphere holds methane and oxygen in the same air, and it is the same sentence: a difference maintained by work that never stops, visible from orbit, and gone the moment the work does.

Between those two ends: a mitochondrion that kept its books. A person. A household with rent on Thursday. A kitchen holding stock. A chama holding a promise. A network of them trading in the dark. A market with no judges. A biosphere. A star holding itself between gravity and fire.

These are not nine systems. **They are one object at nine scales** — each of them a whole with an inside, each of them a part of something larger, each of them alive exactly as long as it keeps itself in range, and not one of them erasing the parts within it in order to be real.

That is a Sustain.

That is Sustena.

And it was one object the whole time — all the way up, all the way down, with no top and no bottom, which is why the last article says the same thing as the first.
