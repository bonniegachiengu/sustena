There is a wall inside you, about the surface area of a small flat, and on the far side of it live roughly thirty-eight trillion bacteria.

They are not an infection. They are the arrangement. They make vitamins you cannot make, digest fibre you cannot digest, and occupy the ecological space that something nastier would otherwise move into. Pressed up against that wall on your side is the largest concentration of immune tissue in your body — more immune cells than are stationed anywhere else, sitting a few cell-widths from the densest population of foreign organisms on Earth, armed, watching, and mostly, deliberately, doing nothing.

And that is before we get to lunch.

Everything you eat is foreign protein. Every mouthful of rice, every bean, every bite of goat. Your immune system's entire recognition apparatus is built to notice exactly that — protein that is not yours — and it has to wave essentially all of it through, all day, for eighty years, without once deciding that a peanut is an enemy. When it fails at this, we do not call it an immune deficiency. We call it an allergy, and it can kill you in eleven minutes.

Giulia Enders, in *Gut*, is very good on this, and it is the fact that reorganises the whole subject: **your immune system's hard problem is not killing things. Killing things is easy. The hard problem is restraint.**

*(One honest correction to a number you may have read in that book and everywhere else: the famous claim that bacterial cells outnumber your own ten to one turns out to be a 1970s back-of-envelope that everybody copied. Sender, Fuchs and Milo re-did the arithmetic properly in 2016 and got roughly one to one — about 38 trillion of them, about 30 trillion of you. Still an astonishing number of lodgers. Just not ten flatmates each.)*

#### **The wall is made of slime, and it is the best thing about you**

Start at the outside, because the outside is where most of the work is done and it gets almost no credit.

Your skin is not really an organ of touch. It is a dead, dry, salty, faintly acidic sheet of overlapping corpses — keratinocytes that killed themselves on purpose and flattened into shingles — and the reason it works is that almost nothing that wants to live in you can live *there*. It is a desert with a wall around it.

Your gut cannot do that. Your gut has to be wet, warm, thin enough to absorb sugar molecules, and lined with living cells. So it does something cleverer and considerably more disgusting.

It makes mucus. Two kinds, in two layers, and in 2008 Malin Johansson and Gunnar Hansson's group in Gothenburg showed exactly how they differ. The outer layer of colonic mucus is loose, and it is *full* of bacteria — a shag-pile carpet of sugar chains that your microbes graze on and burrow into and call home. The inner layer, sitting directly on your cells, is dense, tightly cross-linked, and — this is the astonishing part — essentially **sterile**. Bacteria cannot get into it. The wall is a slime with a public floor and a private floor, and the private floor is completely empty.

Up in the small intestine, tucked into the base of each little crypt, sit Paneth cells, which do nothing but manufacture antimicrobial peptides and spray them into the neighbourhood. And plated over all of it, secreted into the gut by the gram — you make more of this antibody than all other antibodies combined — is secretory IgA, which mostly does not kill bacteria at all. It coats them, clumps them, and keeps them politely at arm's length in the outer layer where they belong.

A wall that kills, a wall that repels, and a wall that just very firmly asks things to stand further back. All of it rebuilt continuously, all of it costing energy every second of your life, all of it silent unless it fails.

#### **The half that was born knowing**

Now suppose something gets through.

The first response is fast — minutes — and it is *fixed*. It does not learn, it was never taught, and you cannot talk it out of it. It is called innate immunity, and for most of the twentieth century immunologists treated it as the boring bit: the janitorial staff, the non-specific soup, the stuff that happens before the real immune system shows up.

In 1989, Charles Janeway Jr. gave a lecture at Cold Spring Harbor that turned out to be one of the most consequential things anyone said in immunology that decade. His argument was roughly: you have all been ignoring the interesting question. How does the adaptive system *know* when to switch on? Something must be telling it "this is dangerous" — and that something must recognise danger it has never met before. Therefore the body must carry inherited receptors that recognise not specific enemies but **classes** of enemy: molecular patterns that microbes make and human cells never do.

He was right, and the proof arrived from a fruit fly. In 1996 Jules Hoffmann's group in Strasbourg found that flies with a broken *Toll* gene — a gene already known for shaping the embryo — died horribly of fungal infection, overgrown with mould. In 1997 Ruslan Medzhitov and Janeway found the human version. In 1998 Bruce Beutler's lab showed that a strain of mice famously unbothered by bacterial endotoxin had a single broken receptor: TLR4. Hoffmann and Beutler shared the Nobel Prize for it in 2011.

What those receptors read is beautifully chosen. The sugar-lipid coat of gram-negative bacteria. The protein flagellin, which is what a bacterial tail is made of. Double-stranded RNA, which almost nothing but a replicating virus produces. DNA with a particular unmethylated signature that yours does not have. These are not enemy faces. They are **things a bacterium cannot stop being without ceasing to be a bacterium.** You did not learn them. You inherited them. Your great-grandmother had the same list, and so will your great-granddaughter.

Which gives innate immunity its defining and slightly frightening property: **it is not editable.** No experience adds to the list. No infection removes anything from it. There is no version of you that gets talked into ignoring flagellin. It is fast because it never has to think, and it never has to think because it was decided long before you existed.

#### **The half that learns, and never lets go**

The other half is slow, and it is the one everybody knows about, because it is the one vaccines are for.

Around 430 BCE, Thucydides watched plague go through Athens and noticed something nobody could explain for another twenty-three centuries: the people who recovered were the ones who could nurse the sick, "for the same man was never attacked twice — never at least fatally." The body remembered.

Adaptive immunity works by carrying an absurd library. Your genome does not have enough genes to spell out a receptor for every molecule you might ever meet — nowhere close. So each developing lymphocyte cuts up and re-splices its own receptor genes, essentially at random, generating a receptor nobody designed. Susumu Tonegawa worked out how, and got the 1987 Nobel for it. Between them, your lymphocytes carry a diversity of receptors far larger than your entire genome could ever encode — a library written by dice.

Then the world arrives and picks. A cell whose random receptor happens to fit an invader gets told to multiply, and it does, and its descendants inherit the fit. That is Macfarlane Burnet's clonal selection, from 1959, and it belongs properly to another article in this series — but the immediate consequence belongs here: **the response is specific, it takes about a week the first time, and afterwards it is kept.**

Kept for how long? Ian Amanna, Mark Slifka and colleagues measured it in 2007 by following people for decades. Antibody responses to measles and mumps and smallpox turned out to have half-lives of two hundred years and up — which is to say, for the practical purposes of a human being, permanent. Edward Jenner scratched cowpox into James Phipps' arm in 1796 on nothing but a rumour about milkmaids, and the reason it worked is that the boy's immune system filed something and never threw it away.

So you have two defences that fail in completely different ways. The innate one is instant and cannot learn, so a genuinely novel trick beats it. The adaptive one learns anything but takes a week, so speed beats it. Neither can cover the other's weakness, which is exactly why you have both, and — hold onto this, it matters later — **they cover for each other only because they work by different machinery.** Two copies of the same detector would not have been a second layer. It would have been the same layer, twice, failing at the same time.

#### **The hardest part: the school where almost everyone dies**

Here is the problem with a library written by dice.

If you generate receptors at random, a great many of them will fit *you*. Your insulin. Your myelin. Your thyroid. The random library has no way of knowing which shapes are yours, because randomness has no way of knowing anything. So the body has to check every single one, by hand, before letting it out.

That checking happens in the thymus, a small organ behind your breastbone that is at its largest when you are a child and then quietly withers. Immature T cells arrive, are shown pieces of self, and are killed if they bind too strongly. The overwhelming majority of the cells that enter never leave. The thymus is a school with an attrition rate that would close any actual school, and its entire curriculum is *don't touch this, don't touch this, don't touch this either*.

But how does a gland behind your sternum show a T cell your pancreas?

In 2002, Mark Anderson, Diane Mathis and Christophe Benoist's groups answered that, and it is one of my favourite facts in biology. A gene called *AIRE* makes the cells of the thymus express, promiscuously, genes belonging to organs all over the body — insulin, and eye proteins, and myelin, and the rest. The thymus performs an impression of your entire body, so that a T cell can be tested against a liver it will never visit. People born with a broken *AIRE* gene get a savage inherited autoimmune syndrome, because the impression was never staged and the graduates never sat the exam.

That is called central tolerance, and it is not enough on its own, because plenty of self never shows up in the thymus, and because — see the top of this article — an enormous amount of harmless *non*-self shows up in the gut three times a day.

So there is a second system, out in the tissues, and its job is active restraint. Some self-reactive cells are switched into anergy — present, alive, functionally muzzled. And crucially there is an entire dedicated cell lineage whose only job is to suppress other immune cells: regulatory T cells, characterised by Shimon Sakaguchi in 1995 and pinned to the transcription factor Foxp3 in 2003. They are the brake. Not the absence of an accelerator — an actual brake, with its own fuel supply, pressed continuously.

You can see what the brake is worth by looking at children born without it. A mutation in *FOXP3* causes IPEX syndrome: catastrophic, multi-organ autoimmunity in infancy. Nothing is wrong with their defences. Their defences work beautifully. That is the problem.

And in the gut this restraint becomes a positive skill with a name. Merrill Chase showed in 1946 that feeding an animal a substance *before* exposing it made the animal tolerate it — **oral tolerance**, the body actively learning not to react to food. Your gut does not merely fail to attack your dinner. It runs a whole education programme teaching itself not to.

Restraint, in other words, is not what is left over when aggression is absent. Restraint is a system, with its own cells, its own genes, its own diseases, and its own budget.

#### **When the brake fails**

Now the failure mode, and I want to be precise about it, because the imprecise version — "the immune system attacks the body" — makes it sound like a malfunction of the weapons.

It isn't. In autoimmune disease the weapons work perfectly. Antibodies form correctly. T cells are recruited correctly, arrive on time, and destroy the target with exemplary efficiency. Everything downstream of the decision is flawless.

**What fails is the classification.** Something got labelled *enemy* that was you.

Type 1 diabetes is the immune system doing an excellent job of eliminating the beta cells of your pancreas. Multiple sclerosis is a well-executed campaign against the insulation on your own nerves. Rheumatoid arthritis, Hashimoto's, coeliac disease, vitiligo, lupus. Roughly four in five people who have one of these is a woman, for reasons still genuinely under investigation. And in every case the sentence to hold is the same: *the defence worked; the classification was wrong.*

Then there is the version where the classification is wrong about something harmless rather than something internal, which is allergy. A peanut is not you and never will be. A peanut is also not a threat. The immune system that kills a child over a trace of peanut protein in a biscuit has correctly identified non-self and catastrophically misjudged what to do about it.

Which tells you the shape of the whole problem. A defence that never refuses anything is useless. A defence that refuses too much is a disease. And there is no setting that makes both numbers zero, ever, in any system, biological or otherwise — you can only decide which error you would rather make and pay for it knowingly.

#### **The disguise**

Pathogens, of course, know all this, in the only way evolution knows anything.

If the check is *does this look like me*, then the winning move is to look like you. The technical name is molecular mimicry, and the classic case is one your grandmother probably knew by its consequences. A *Streptococcus* throat infection carries a surface protein whose shape resembles cardiac myosin, the motor protein of your heart muscle. Some people's immune systems, having correctly learned to attack the strep, go on attacking the resemblance — and that is rheumatic fever, which is why an untreated sore throat could, in the era before antibiotics, quietly ruin a heart valve.

Robert Fujinami and Michael Oldstone laid out the general mechanism in 1985. There is a bacterium, *Campylobacter jejuni*, whose sugar coat resembles a ganglioside on your own nerves — get the wrong strain from the wrong chicken and you can end up paralysed with Guillain-Barré syndrome. And in 2022 two remarkable papers landed on multiple sclerosis: Kjetil Bjornevik's group showed, across a decade of blood samples from ten million US military personnel, that infection with Epstein-Barr virus raises MS risk about thirty-two-fold and essentially always precedes it; William Robinson's group showed antibodies against one EBV protein cross-reacting with a protein in the human brain.

A pathogen that looks like self is not defeating your defences. It is *using* them — using the one check the whole system is built around, by presenting the right credentials.

#### **And the check itself was always the wrong question**

Which brings us to the argument that has been running quietly under everything above.

"Self versus non-self" is how immunology described itself for fifty years, and as a description of what your body actually does it is not very good. Your gut tolerates thirty-eight trillion foreign organisms and a lifetime of foreign food. A pregnant woman carries, for nine months, a person half of whose proteins came from someone else — Peter Medawar in 1953 called this the immunological paradox of pregnancy, and it is still a fair name. Meanwhile the same system will, in about one person in ten, mount a beautifully specific attack on a thyroid gland that has been theirs since birth.

Enormous non-self tolerated. Some self destroyed. As a rule, *is it me?* predicts the behaviour badly.

In 1994 Polly Matzinger proposed the alternative, and it caused a satisfying amount of shouting. What if the immune system is not asking whether something is foreign, but whether anything is being **damaged**? Cells that die cleanly, by the orderly programmed route, are cleared in silence. Cells that die messily — burst, necrotic, spilling their insides where insides do not belong — release signals that mean *something has gone wrong here*, and it is those signals, not foreignness, that license a response.

On that reading, food is tolerated because eating hurts nobody. Gut bacteria are tolerated because, in the outer mucus where they belong, they hurt nobody. And the immune system attacks not what is strange but what arrives in a context of harm.

The danger model is not settled, and immunologists still argue about how much of it survives contact with the details. But the *criticism* is unarguable, and it is the sentence to carry out of the biology: **identity alone is not a sufficient basis for a decision. You also have to know what is being done, and to what.**

None of that was a metaphor, and this is where it lands.

The last articles built a region a system must stay inside, the only moves that may change it, the law that makes the region binding, the record that holds everything in order, and the wall where the world is converted before it enters. Read them again with an immunologist's eye and you notice something: **there is no security module anywhere, and there never needed to be one.** Security is not a component of Sustena. It is a property of two things Sustena already has.

A Sustain is `Σ = ⟨B, S, V, T, ⊕⟩`. The **B** is the barrier. The gate on **T** is the check. That is the entire defensive apparatus, and everything else is those two things repeated at every scale.

**The barrier is real and it is doing the unglamorous work.** Sustena's boundary already converts rather than admits: an M-Pesa SMS becomes a typed event candidate at the wall and the raw words stay outside. And there is exactly one thing dropped on purpose, never parsed, never stored — a message carrying a one-time code. That drop is checked on the phone before the message ever leaves the device, checked again in the server's intake before anything touches a database, and checked a third time before any parser sees it. Skin, mucus, and the sterile inner layer.

**That drop is innate immunity, in the strict sense.** It is fixed code. It is not a rule anyone authored and it is not a rule anyone can retire. The codebase says so out loud, in the module that defines the *learnable* rules: *no parse rule can ever be authored, corrected, generated, or retired to weaken it; it stays fixed code, checked before any rule runs.* That is the germline. You cannot train your way out of recognising flagellin, and no amount of learning inside Sustena can teach it to store an OTP.

**And the learned rules are the adaptive half.** A parse rule that arrives because a person corrected a misreading is tagged `user_corrected`; one that shipped with the system is tagged `shipped`; a machine-proposed rule must survive verification against every existing rule's own examples before a human is ever offered it, and a candidate that misfires is rejected rather than adopted. Slow, specific, learned from experience, and — exactly like a thymocyte — tested against the existing self before being allowed out.

**The gate is the check that self-vs-non-self was always meant to be**, and Sustena's version is better than the immunological original, because it never had the luxury of asking only *are you me?* It asks four things at once: is this a legal move, is this a legal result, is this a legal step — and *who is asking*. Identity and authority as separate questions. Matzinger's correction, built in from the start, because a household ledger was never going to survive on foreignness alone.

**The log is immune memory.** Every event, in order, append-only, never overwritten — the substrate on which "this has happened before" and "this has never happened before" both become answerable questions.

**Defence in depth is not a layer count. It is nested Sustains.** A pocket inside a treasury inside a household inside a business: each one a Sustain, each one with its own boundary and its own gate, each gate answering for its own scale. Permission is the *and* of all of them, and it stays person-first — the parts are not dissolved by the whole they belong to. That is not a security architecture bolted on. It is `⊕`, which was already there.

And now the honest part, because this series does not do triumphant.

**Over-refusal is the failure mode with a name.** A system that blocks a legitimate spend because a rule was written slightly too tight is not being careful. It is being autoimmune — attacking its own user's correct action with perfect efficiency and complete confidence. Sustena's design has been quietly guarding against this from early on: a parent's rules are *advisory by default*, and a binding parent rule refuses a child's move only if that move would **newly** break it, so a household already over budget does not thereby lose the ability to act. That is a Foxp3 cell in the architecture: an explicit brake, chosen on purpose, costing something.

**Two copies of the same filter are not two layers.** The phone-side OTP check and the server-side OTP check run the *same list of keyword patterns*, kept in step by hand across two languages. That genuinely defends against one thing — a message that reaches the server by some route that skips the app — and it defends against nothing at all in the case that matters more: a one-time code phrased in a way neither list anticipated slips through both, at the same instant, for the same reason. Your body did not make that mistake. Innate and adaptive immunity fail differently because they *recognise* differently, and that difference is the whole value of having two.

**And there is a hole in the skin.** The gate is real, it runs on the candidate state before anything is written, and it refuses with a named reason. But it is switched on per Sustain rather than always, and there is a second way to write state that skips it by default. A gate that most moves go through is not a gate — it is a strong suggestion. The wall around your brain does not have a second entrance for trusted molecules.

Which leaves the sentence the article rests on.

A defence is not the part that attacks. **It is a boundary, one gate, and a memory — repeated at every scale, and the hardest of the three is knowing when to say yes.** The killing is the easy part. Every immune system on Earth could kill more than it does. What separates a body that is alive from a body eating itself is a trillion small decisions, every second, to let something through.
