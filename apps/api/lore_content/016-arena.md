There is a trade going on under your feet right now, and it has been going on for about four hundred and fifty million years.

A plant root, growing through soil, is very good at one thing and very bad at another. It is good at catching sunlight and turning it into sugar. It is bad at finding phosphorus, because phosphorus in soil barely moves — it binds to mineral surfaces and sits there, sometimes a millimetre from a root that cannot reach it. A fungus has the opposite problem and the opposite gift. Its hyphae are a hundred times finer than a root hair and can thread into pores a root will never enter, so it can find phosphorus almost anywhere. What it cannot do is photosynthesise. It has no way to make sugar at all.

So they trade. The fungus grows into the root's cells, builds a branched exchange structure inside them — the arbuscule, a tree-shaped thing that exists purely to increase surface area — and carbon flows one way while phosphorus flows the other.

That much has been known since the 1880s, when Albert Bernhard Frank looked at the root tips of forest trees and argued, against a fair amount of scorn, that the fungus wrapping them was not a disease.

Here is the part that took another century and a quarter.

---

#### **The experiment that turned a symbiosis into a market**

The obvious worry about any exchange like this is the obvious worry about any exchange. What stops one side from taking and not giving?

A fungus that collects sugar and delivers no phosphorus is doing better, in the short run, than one that delivers. Its sugar is free. If cheating pays, cheaters should spread, and if cheaters spread, the whole arrangement should have collapsed sometime in the Devonian. It did not collapse. It became the foundation of terrestrial plant life. Something is holding it together.

In 2011, Toby Kiers and a large group of colleagues published the answer in *Science*, and the method is the beautiful part. They did not just watch a plant and a fungus. They gave each side a **choice**.

They grew roots that were connected to more than one fungal partner at once, where the partners differed in how much phosphorus they actually delivered — some generous, some stingy. And they did the mirror experiment too: a single fungal network reaching into more than one root compartment, where the compartments differed in how much carbon they could supply.

Then they labelled the goods with isotopes, so every atom could be followed, and they measured who sent what to whom.

The result is one sentence and it changes the category of the thing:

**The plant sent more carbon to the fungus that delivered more phosphorus. And the fungus sent more phosphorus to the root that delivered more carbon.**

Both directions. Reciprocal. Each side was measuring its partners against each other and reallocating toward the better one.

Sit with what is absent from that sentence. There is no nervous system on either side. No memory in any sense you would recognise. No contract, no court, no reputation score, no third party. There is a root and a fungus and a rule about where to put the next unit of carbon, and out of that rule comes something that behaves — measurably, in isotope counts — like a market clearing.

---

#### **Not a metaphor. A model that happens to fit both.**

It is tempting to call this "like a market" and move on, and that would be the wrong move, because the market reading is not decoration here. It is the framework that predicted the result.

Ronald Noë and Peter Hammerstein set it out in 1994 under the name **biological market theory**. Their claim was that whenever you find a certain configuration in nature, you should expect market behaviour, and you should expect it for market reasons:

- two classes of trader, offering **different** commodities;
- **partner choice** — each side can pick who to deal with;
- **outside options** — the exchange rate is set by what else is available;
- **competition within a class** — traders outbid each other for partners.

Notice that no cognition appears in that list. Nothing there requires a brain. It requires only that allocation respond to returns, and evolution is extremely good at producing allocation rules that respond to returns.

The mycorrhizal symbiosis has all four. And once you see it as a market, you can ask market questions of it and get market answers. In 2019 the same research group tracked phosphorus through fungal networks using fluorescent tags and found the fungus doing something a trader would recognise instantly: it moved phosphorus **from patches where it was abundant to patches where it was scarce** — carrying its goods toward the roots where they commanded the better price in carbon. Not sharing. Arbitrage.

And the cheating problem? Kiers and colleagues had already shown the harder version of the mechanism in 2003, in legumes and their nitrogen-fixing bacteria: a plant given nodules that *stopped fixing nitrogen* cut off their oxygen supply, and those bacteria reproduced far less. That is a **sanction** — a cost imposed on a partner that stopped delivering.

In the mycorrhizal case the sanction is gentler and, honestly, should be described precisely: it is not punishment, it is **withheld reward**. The stingy fungus does not get hurt. It just gets less. But less carbon means less growth, less growth means less network, less network means fewer partners next season, and across enough seasons "gets less" and "is punished" converge to the same thing.

That is the whole design, and it is three moves:

**Measure what a partner actually delivered. Reward in proportion. Withhold from the ones who didn't.**

No judge. No contract. No trust in anybody's good character. Just allocation that follows delivery, applied by both sides at once.

#### **One honest caveat, because the story has been oversold elsewhere**

You have probably met a bigger version of this claim — the "wood wide web," forests wired into cooperative networks, mother trees deliberately feeding their seedlings. Some of that is real and some of it has run well ahead of the evidence. A 2023 review by Justine Karst, Melanie Jones and Jason Hoeksema went through the citation trail and found positive-citation bias and overinterpretation running through the popular account of common mycorrhizal networks in forests.

I am not leaning on the forest story. I am leaning on the dish.

And the dish is the stronger claim, not the weaker one, because in the dish they controlled who could trade with whom, they labelled the goods, and they counted. What survives that scrutiny is not a poetic image of a cooperating forest. It is a measured, reciprocal, sanction-stabilised exchange between two organisms with no minds — which is a much stranger fact, and a much more useful one.

Because it means market design is not a human invention. It is a solution that gets found repeatedly, by anything that has to allocate a scarce good among partners of unequal quality.

Which brings this home.

#### **The Arena**

Sustena is built out of one thing repeated: a **Sustain** — a boundary, a state, a region it has to stay inside, a set of gated moves, and the ability to compose with other Sustains. A household is one. A person is one. A business is one. The network that connects them — **Mycelium** — is one too, which was the last article's punchline.

And Sustains, once there are many of them, start making things worth having.

Somebody writes an **Enzyme** — a guarded move — that parses M-Pesa messages properly, and it works. Somebody tunes a **Symbiont** — a semi-autonomous adviser — that watches a small farm's crop calendar and gets good at it. Somebody assembles a **spore**: a whole Sustain definition, a Genome packaged so that it can germinate somewhere else. Somebody builds a **widget** that shows a month's burn rate in a way you actually understand at a glance.

None of that should have to be built twice.

The **Arena** is where those things are published, priced, found, judged, and chosen. Enzymes, Symbionts, spores, widgets, Genomes, memes. Priced in **pawa** and **Juul**. Riding on Mycelium — which handles the addressing, the routing, and the settlement — so the Arena itself only has to do the thing a market does.

And now the mycorrhizal result stops being a nice opening story and starts being the specification.

Because the Arena has all four of Noë and Hammerstein's conditions, exactly. Two classes of trader — publishers and adopters. Different commodities — competence for currency. Partner choice on both sides. Outside options setting the rate. The fungus and the root are not an analogy for the Arena. **They are the same model, running on different hardware.**

Which means the Arena inherits the cheating problem, in full, with none of the four hundred and fifty million years of tuning.

#### **The market for lemons, which is the thing to be afraid of**

In 1970, George Akerlof wrote a short paper about used cars that got him a Nobel Prize, and its argument is the single most important thing to hold in mind about any marketplace of things you cannot inspect before you buy.

Suppose some used cars are good and some are bad, the seller knows which, and the buyer does not. What will a buyer pay? Not the price of a good car — they might get a bad one. They will pay something like the average.

But now look at what that does to the sellers. The owner of a genuinely good car is being offered the average, which is less than the car is worth. So they don't sell. They withdraw.

And the moment they withdraw, the average quality of what remains on the market **falls**. Which lowers what buyers will pay. Which pushes out the next tier of good sellers. Which lowers the average again.

It runs downhill on its own. The end state is a market where only the worst goods trade, or no market at all.

Read the failure mode carefully, because it is almost always misread. The problem is not "some bad packages get published." Bad packages are survivable; you find them, you stop using them. The problem is **the good contributors leave**. It is a shift in the whole distribution, driven by the fact that quality could not be seen, and the people it hurts most are exactly the ones who were doing the best work.

An Arena where you cannot tell a good Symbiont from a bad one before you install it is a lemons market. Not eventually. Structurally, from the first day it has more than a handful of entries.

So the market needs the fungus's three moves.

**Measure what was actually delivered.** After the fact, from real outcomes, per version — not the author's description, and not a number somebody typed.

**Reward in proportion.** Adoption, ranking, and the share of the price that flows back to whoever built the thing.

**Withhold from the ones who didn't.** Demotion, price escalation, suspension of the right to publish — graduated, reversible, and appealable, because a market that cannot sanction is a market that rots, and a market that sanctions without appeal is worse.

#### **The trap on the other side: popularity is not quality**

Here is where it gets subtle, and where a second experiment is worth the detour.

The obvious way to measure a market's opinion is to count. Downloads. Installs. Stars. The crowd has spoken.

In 2006, Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds and Duncan Watts built an artificial music market to test exactly that. Fourteen thousand participants, unknown songs by unknown bands, download counts recorded. The trick was that they secretly split the participants into **eight parallel worlds**, each with its own independent download counter, each starting from zero.

If popularity tracks quality, the eight worlds should broadly agree. The good songs should rise in all of them.

They did not agree. A song that finished near the top in one world could finish near the bottom in another. Early random fluctuations — the first few people who happened to click — got amplified by everyone who came after and looked at the counter. And when the researchers turned the social signal *up*, two things rose together: **inequality** (hits got hittier) and **unpredictability** (which song became the hit got less forecastable).

Quality was not irrelevant. The very best songs rarely bombed, and the very worst rarely triumphed. But in the whole broad middle, where most things live, outcome was substantially a matter of who was early.

**Quality bounds the outcome. It does not determine it.**

That is the second failure mode, and it is the mirror of the first. Akerlof says: if buyers can't see quality, the market fills with the worst. Salganik says: if buyers can only see *what other buyers did*, the market fills with whatever got lucky first. Both are failures of the same missing thing — **evidence about the artefact itself**, as opposed to evidence about the crowd.

And it is why the fungus's rule is subtler than "follow the crowd." The plant does not send its carbon to the fungus with the most connections. It sends carbon to the fungus that **delivered phosphorus to it**. First-hand, measured, per-partner. That is not a popularity contest. It is an audit.

#### **And why there is no such thing as the best**

One more, and then the landing.

In 1997 David Wolpert and William Macready proved something with an unforgettable name: the **No Free Lunch** theorem. Averaged across *all possible problems*, every search strategy performs exactly the same. There is no algorithm that is better in general, because "in general" includes all the problems your algorithm is bad at, and they exactly cancel the ones it is good at.

The consequence for a marketplace is direct and slightly brutal. **A global "top packages" ranking is a claim about the average of all possible problems, and nobody lives there.**

A budgeting Symbiont tuned for a salaried household with predictable monthly income is not merely *less good* for a market trader whose income arrives daily in unequal lumps — it is built on an assumption that is false there. Ranking it first for both is not a small inaccuracy. It is a category error.

What makes a library valuable is not that it contains the best things. It is that a particular kind of life has a particular, **lopsided** distribution of problems, and the library is shaped to that lopsidedness.

Sustena already has the word for that shape. A **Niche** is a viable region seen as a place to live. So the ranking has to be per-Niche, and this is not a preference — it is forced by a theorem.

#### **The last turn**

Everything above is about a marketplace. So it would be natural to picture the Arena as a layer sitting *above* all the Sustains — the platform, the exchange, the thing the participants come to.

That is the picture to give up, and giving it up is the point of the whole series.

The Arena has a **boundary**: who may publish, who may buy. It has a **state**: the catalogue, the prices, the trust, the orders. It has a **region it must stay inside**: nothing counterfeit, provenance intact, trust earned rather than asserted, and every order conserving — what leaves one account arrives in others, exactly. It has a set of **gated moves**: publish, price, rate, order, sanction. And it has **Symbionts** living inside it — curators, market-makers — which, like every Symbiont anywhere in Sustena, advise and never decide.

Boundary. State. Viable region. Transitions. Composition.

That is a **Sustain**. The same five-part object as the household, as the person, as the network.

The Arena is not above the Sustains trading in it. It is one of them — a marketplace Sustain, riding on Mycelium the way a habitat sits inside a homestead, contained by the network, containing nothing but a catalogue and its rules. One more turn of the same recursion.

And that is not a tidy philosophical flourish. It is the load-bearing safety property, and here is why.

Because the Arena is a Sustain and not a platform, it has no privileged reach into anything that buys from it. When a spore is purchased, it does not get *installed* by some special mechanism the market controls. It **germinates** — it is composed into the buyer's Sustain by the identical move that composes a habitat into a homestead, and it passes through the buyer's own gate, under the buyer's own law, exactly like every other change to that household's state.

Which means the market cannot recall what it has sold, and does not need to. It can stop listing an artefact. It can stop settling on it. It can sanction its author. But the thing that protects the buyer was never the catalogue's judgement.

**It was the gate on their own door.**

The plant does not trust the fungus. It measures it, and it holds the carbon.
