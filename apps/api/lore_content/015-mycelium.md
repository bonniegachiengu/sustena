In the summer of 1993, a young forester was kneeling in a clear-cut in British Columbia, tying plastic bags over saplings.

Suzanne Simard was not looking for a metaphor. She was looking for an accounting error. The Forest Service had a policy: to grow good Douglas-fir, kill the paper birch. Birch was a weed. It stole light, it stole water, it stole soil. So crews went through the cut blocks with herbicide and brush saws and removed it — and the fir, freed from its competitor, kept dying anyway.

Simard's question was small and unfashionable. What if the birch wasn't only taking?

---

#### The experiment

Here is what she did, and the design is the whole argument.

She planted triplets: a paper birch, a Douglas-fir, and a western redcedar, growing together in the field. Then she bagged them and fed each one labelled carbon dioxide — a heavy isotope for the birch, a radioactive one for the fir — so that any carbon fixed by one tree could later be found, and identified, inside the other.

The cedar was the control, and it was the cleverest part. Birch and fir both partner with the same family of fungi. Cedar partners with a different one entirely. If carbon moved between trees through the soil at random — leaking out of roots, diffusing through dirt — the cedar should have picked up its share. If carbon moved through the *fungus*, the cedar was locked out.

The carbon moved. Both directions, and net toward the fir. Cedar got almost nothing.

Then she shaded the fir. And the shaded fir — the one that could not feed itself — received more.

---

#### The thing underneath

What she had found a way to see was already famous in soil biology and invisible to everyone else: a fungal fabric threading the whole forest floor. Hyphae finer than root hairs, wrapping tree roots, tunnelling into them, running metres through the dark, joining one tree to the next and the next.

A decade later, Kevin Beiler's team went and mapped one. They pulled DNA from a stand of Douglas-fir and reconstructed which fungal individual touched which tree. What came out wasn't a mesh. It was a *topology*. Most trees had a few connections. A handful of old, big trees had dozens. The forest, drawn honestly, looked like a network diagram — hubs and spokes, the pattern you get in airline routes and power grids, the pattern engineers spend careers trying to design on purpose.

Somebody called it the wood-wide web, and the name stuck, and that is roughly where the story usually stops: the wise old mother tree, feeding her children through the dark.

---

#### It's a market

Except the fungus is not a charity, and this is the part worth slowing down for.

The fungus is a trader. It cannot photosynthesise — it has no way to make sugar — so it buys carbon from trees. In exchange it sells what it is superb at getting: phosphorus and nitrogen, scavenged from soil at a resolution no root can reach. Both sides are exploiting the other's competence. That is what mutualism is.

And in 2011 Toby Kiers' group showed the exchange has *prices*. Working with plants and fungi in a controlled system, they found the plant sends more carbon to the fungal partners delivering more phosphorus — and the fungus sends more phosphorus to the more generous host. Both sides discriminate. Both sides can sanction a partner that under-delivers. Biologists have a name for it: a biological market. Supply, demand, partner choice, and a penalty for cheating, running in soil, with no one in charge.

So the wood-wide web is a commons made of self-interested members. Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing.

---

#### The honest part

I have to put one thing down before going further, because leaving it out would be a cheat.

In 2023, Justine Karst and colleagues went back through the literature on forest fungal networks and found that the popular version had outrun the evidence. Citations had drifted; hedged findings had hardened into facts; the nurturing mother tree had become a story the field was telling itself. The networks are real. Carbon moves. Whether it moves in amounts that matter to a forest's fate is genuinely unsettled.

I am keeping the analogy anyway, and keeping the caveat with it, because what I want from the forest is not proof. It is a shape. The shape is: *autonomous members, a shared fabric between them, resources and signals routed across it, a market riding on top, and a subsidy that sometimes flows to whoever is in the shade.* That shape is real whatever the ecology turns out to say. And in Sustena, unlike in a forest, we don't have to infer the subsidy from an isotope. We get to declare it.

#### Now the bridge

In Sustena, everything is one recursive thing — a **Sustain**: a boundary, a state, a region it must stay inside, the moves it can make, and a way of composing with others. A person has one: their **habitat**. A household is one: a **homestead**, with those habitats inside it. An organisation is one, with many stakeholders inside it. Each is complete. Each is sovereign over its own rules. Each can stand alone.

Those are the trees.

**Mycelium** is the fabric between them. It carries three kinds of traffic, and the distinction matters more than it looks:

**Energy.** **Pawa** is what a Sustain spends to do anything — every gated move, every Symbiont's deliberation, every model call, metered. Pawa is conserved: routing it means moving it, never copying it.

**Signal.** Events, published into the fabric and picked up by whoever subscribed. A farm broadcasting that it has maize; a household's Symbiont hearing it. Information is the opposite of energy — it *should* be copied, and the discipline is making sure a fact arriving twice only lands once.

**Competence.** Shared **Symbionts** and shared **memes** — the strategies, templates and patterns one Sustain has learned that another can adopt. The fungus that already knows where the phosphorus is.

Mycelium also holds a purse — the **network treasury** — and carries a market on its back: the **Arena**, where memes, templates and Symbionts are published, rated by trust and provenance, and selected. And it has its own hub trees. The **Mycelium Foundation** runs the Onboarding Grant: a hundred pawa to every new user, out of the commons, so that nobody's first week is spent unable to afford a thought. The **Sustena Institute** does the research the whole network benefits from and no single member would fund.

Neither of them gets special powers. They propose, and a gate decides, exactly like every other Symbiont in the system. A mother tree with override privileges would not be a forest. It would be a plantation.

#### The reduction

Here is the line the whole article exists for.

The forest floor is not a thing *above* the trees. It is not a layer of management. It is more roots and more fungus — the same biology, at a bigger scale.

Mycelium is not a thing above the Sustains. **It is itself a Sustain, whose members are Sustains.** Its Boundary is membership — who is in the network. Its State includes the treasury. Its Enzymes are the gated moves that route, publish and settle. The Foundation and the Institute are its Symbionts, whose niche happens to be the whole graph. The Arena is a market inside it.

Nothing new was introduced. The network is the same recursive object as a pantry shelf, wearing the largest coat below Sustena itself.

Which leaves exactly one Sustain still above it. That one gets the last article.
