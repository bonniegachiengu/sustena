In 1994 two Swedish biologists set out to embarrass their own side of an argument.

Dan-Eric Nilsson and Susanne Pelger wanted to know how long it takes to evolve an eye, and they deliberately stacked every assumption against a quick answer. They began with the crudest possible starting point — a flat patch of light-sensitive cells between two layers of tissue — and allowed change in steps of one percent at a time. The patch curves. The curve deepens into a cup. The cup narrows toward a pinhole. The transparent filling stiffens and refracts. One thousand eight hundred and twenty-nine steps later they had a fish's lens eye, and their pessimistic estimate came to a few hundred thousand generations. A geological instant.

The number is famous. The constraint that produced it is the interesting part.

Every one of those 1,829 intermediate eyes had to be a *better* eye than the one before it. Not a worse eye on the way to a better one. Not a scrambled eye for eighty generations while the lens arrived. At no point in the sequence was the animal permitted to be blind.

That is not a rigour the authors imposed on themselves for the sake of a strong result. It is imposed by the animal, which has to go on living the entire time.

---

#### **The mountain with no cliff route**

Richard Dawkins gave the picture its name in 1996. Imagine a mountain with a sheer cliff on one face and a long gentle slope around the back. Complex organs sit at the summit. The cliff is the single leap that produces them; the slope is the only route anything ever took.

And the reason the cliff is closed is not arithmetic. It is not that the leap is improbable. It is that a leap requires a leaper, and the leaper has to survive the landing — and the landing is a whole working organism, not a change.

Darwin had already stated the test that would destroy his own theory: an organ that could not have been formed by numerous, successive, slight modifications. Then he went and did the eye himself, in the *Origin*, listing the living intermediates that were sitting in front of him all along.

---

#### **Gradual is not the same as slow**

This gets confused, so it is worth separating.

Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould argued in 1972 that the fossil record shows long stretches of stasis broken by bursts of rapid change. That is a claim about **rate**, and everything above survives it intact. You may cross the slope quickly. You may cross it in a hurry, under pressure, in a single geological blink.

What no version of the argument permits is a dead step. A large simultaneous redesign is lethal not because it is fast but because it is *large and simultaneous* — it is precisely the kind of change least likely to leave a working organism on the far side of it. A sequence of small viable edits reaching the same destination is not the timid version of the same move. It is the only version that has anyone alive at the end.

---

#### **Development that refuses to be knocked off course**

There is a second thing making the slope walkable, and C. H. Waddington named it in 1942.

He called it **canalization**, and later drew it as a landscape: development as a ball rolling down a surface grooved into valleys. The valleys are the whole point. A small nudge does not move the outcome sideways. The ball gets carried back to the floor of the valley it was already in, and the animal that comes out the other end is the animal that was going to come out anyway.

Which is what makes editing survivable at all. A system that translated every small change to its definition into a proportional change in the thing itself would have no slope to climb — every single step would be an untested organism to be judged from scratch. Buffering means most edits land somewhere the system already knows how to be. The perturbation is carried back into the region, and nothing had to ask.

#### **Changing the structure while it holds the load**

Your skeleton is under load every second of your life, and it is being rebuilt the entire time.

But never all at once. Bone is rebuilt in small packets — one crew of cells removes a parcel of old bone, another lays down new, and Harold Frost named that unit, the basic multicellular unit, in the 1960s. At any given moment the overwhelming majority of the skeleton is intact and carrying weight. The unit is small *because the load never pauses.* You do not get a new skeleton in one night, and the reason is not that biology lacks ambition.

Then there is the crab, which appears to break the rule outright. Arthropods replace their skeleton entirely — the whole shell, in one event. That is the leap the slope was supposed to forbid.

It isn't, and the order of operations is why. The new cuticle is grown underneath the old one first. For a period, both exist. Only then is the old one shed and the new one hardened. The animal is never without a shell. It is briefly wearing two.

Build the new structure beside the old one. Move across. Only then take the old one away.

---

#### **The one condition**

Every one of these has been saying the same sentence, and it is worth writing plainly.

**An edit to a running system is safe if and only if every existing instance survives it.**

Not "the new design is better." Not "the rules are more correct now." Those are questions about the destination, and the destination is the easy part. The condition is about everything currently alive: under the new definition, is each of them still inside the region where it can go on living — or can it be carried there?

That is the migration predicate, in biology's own terms, and biology enforces it the only way anything can. Not by review. By the fact that the ones for whom it failed are not here.

---

None of that was a metaphor.

A Sustena definition is a written document — the genome of the last article — declaring **S**, the dimensions; **V**, the region the thing is alive inside; **T**, the moves it may make. Instances run on it: households, kitchens, pockets, a business. The cell established what V is. The law established that V is binding, because a gate sits on every transition.

Editing is what happens when somebody changes the definition while things are running on it.

The change is versioned, **D → D′**, and the safety condition is exactly biology's. For every live instance, that instance's *actual current state* must still be inside V under the new definition — or a migration must carry it there. An edit that would strand a household outside its own declared viable region is refused, and the refusal names the household and names the rule.

The asymmetry from the slope survives too. Relaxing a rule is free: everyone who was already inside the region is still inside the wider one, and nothing needs checking. Tightening a rule always pays, because tightening is the move that can leave someone outside a wall that wasn't there yesterday.

A reversible edit declares its inverse — and where the migration threw information away, the rollback is reported as **partial**, not pretended total. A skeleton remodelled is not a skeleton you can un-remodel by wanting to.

And an edit is not a back door. It goes through the same gate as every other write — it *is* an Enzyme, one level up, an Enzyme whose subject is the definition — with one addition: changing the rules requires an authority the rules themselves do not grant. Being permitted to spend from a pocket has never made anyone permitted to change what a pocket is. Structurally, editing the rules is harder than following them.

Which leaves the sentence the module rests on. **You cannot quietly break a running household by editing its rules** — not because somebody is watching the diff, but because the edit does not commit. The definition has a gate on it, the same way the state does.

A system that can be safely edited is not one that has been made cautious. It is one where the intermediate step was never allowed to be dead.
