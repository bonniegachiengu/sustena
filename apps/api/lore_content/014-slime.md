Here is something that should not work.

There's a creature on the forest floor with no brain, no nerves, and no one in charge. Alone, it's a single cell — it crawls, eats bacteria, keeps to itself. Starve it, though, and tens of thousands of them will find each other, pour into one body, and crawl away as a single animal. That animal builds a tower out of itself, and one cell in five turns into dead scaffolding so the rest can be flung into the wind. The survivors land, break back into loners, and carry on as if nothing happened.

Nobody gave an order. No cell could see the whole. Yet they moved as one — exactly when it mattered, and only for as long as it mattered.

How do many separate things become one purposeful thing, with no one holding the plan? It's the oldest question in biology, in economies, in families — and Sustena has to answer it for everything it federates: not only people, but Symbionts, compute nodes, storage nodes — any autonomous thing that can stand on its own and, when the moment demands, must act as part of something larger. So let's watch the organism that answers it best.

---

#### **The starving amoeba**

The creature is _Dictyostelium discoideum_, and for most of its life it wants nothing to do with its own kind. It hunts alone. It divides alone. Then the bacteria run out, and hunger flips a switch.

A starving cell releases a single pulse of a chemical — cyclic AMP. A neighbor catches the pulse, crawls toward it, and fires its own. That one triggers the next, and the next, until a wave of signal is rolling outward across the whole population like a heartbeat with no heart. Follow the gradient inward and you get a stampede: tens of thousands of cells, each obeying nothing but the pulse beside it, streaming into one mound.

The mound stands up and starts to move. It's a slug now — a coordinated body that senses light and heat and crawls toward the surface as one. The cells never merged; each keeps its own wall. They simply share a direction.

At the surface comes the part that should stop you. The slug builds a stalk to lift itself into the air, and to build it, one cell in five hardens into a dead column and dies there — spending itself so the others, riding the top as spores, can catch the wind. The spores scatter, land on food, crack open, and each becomes a lone amoeba again. Whole. Independent. Alone.

The many became one, did the work, and became many again.

#### **The cell that maps cities**

A cousin makes the same point from the other direction. _Physarum polycephalum_ is a single cell that never splits into a crowd — it just spreads, a sprawling yellow web with thousands of nuclei and no brain at all. Drop it in a maze with food at both ends and it floods every corridor, then withdraws from the dead ends until only the shortest path between the two meals remains. Lay oat flakes on a map of Tokyo — one flake per city around the capital — and within a day it grows a web of tubes connecting them that matches the real rail network: about as efficient, and harder to break.

No engineer. No plan. No center. Just one body thickening the tubes that carried the most flow and starving the ones that didn't, until what was left was nearly optimal.

The network was the decision.

---

Both organisms run the same program, and it is the program Sustena needs so that many separate things can act as one household, one business, one cluster, one push toward a goal — without any of them dissolving into the group.

Start with the node. In Sustena the smallest sustain is any autonomous whole — a person, a Symbiont, a compute node, a store of data — a complete thing with its own private state, its own work, its own appetite. This is the amoeba. It is self-sufficient by default, and it owes the collective nothing until there is a reason.

The reason is pressure with a shared shape: a rent deadline, a launch week, a savings target the family sets together, a surge of work no single machine can hold. That pressure is the starvation signal, and it travels the way _Dictyostelium_'s does — a pulse that propagates locally, node to node, until the population orients toward one point. That pulse is the Signal primitive we already found hiding inside Monitor, Controller, and Tenet; the Mycelium network is the medium it moves through. Each node chooses to relay the wave, and the aggregation rises out of those local choices rather than any order from above.

When they answer, they compose into a larger sustain — a household body, a business body, a cluster, a quorum — that moves with one will toward the goal. Roles differentiate exactly as stalk and spore do: someone fronts the cash and is spent for the season; someone carries the labour; a node is elected to coordinate while the others do the reaching; one is lifted so it can touch what the rest cannot. Privileges and responsibilities inside the composed whole are simply the cell types — the division of a single effort. And when the goal is met, the body disperses; the members return to their own lives and their own idle, each carrying home what the effort earned, the way spores carry the colony's future to new soil.

Through all of it, the individual never disappears. Even packed inside the slug, every amoeba keeps its own membrane and its own genome. That is the holon, and it is the one law Multiparty cannot break: a composed sustain may direct its members, spend their shared resources, and act in the world as a single body, yet it must leave the node inside it whole. The whole is real. So is each part. Both are true at once, which is the entire difficulty and the entire point.

And like _Physarum_ on its map of Tokyo, the composed sustain works out how to route its shared resources — money, time, tasks, compute, storage — by thickening what carries the most and starving what carries the least, until the allocation is close to the best any planner could draw. Nobody holds the map. The network is the decision.

#### **Two people, and a number between them**

The hardest version of cooperation is not the crowd. It is two.

In a crowd you can be carried. With two people there is nowhere to hide: every time one of you covers something, the other one either eventually covers something back, or does not. Biologists call it reciprocal altruism, and the thing that makes it hold together is embarrassingly simple. Both parties have to be able to see where they stand.

Not a running total of who is the better person. Just a number, with a sign. He sent fare. She bought the shopping. He covered a bill. She sent it back. Add the arrows in one direction, subtract the arrows in the other, and what is left is the relationship as it currently stands.

Here is the part worth pausing on. That number is not enough on its own. A thousand shillings outstanding reads exactly the same whether he sent a thousand once and she never paid, or whether forty thousand has gone back and forth across a year and this is where it happened to land. Same number. Completely different friendship.

So the number is never stored. It is worked out, every time, from the whole history of what actually moved — which means the history can always be asked, and the two of you can always see not just where you stand but how you got there.
